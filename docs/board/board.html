1

The question

Surrey manages 144 green corridors on a limiting budget. Which of them are drying out and should get water, soil work or replanting first?

2

The assumption

It is widely assumed that answering this needs climate data at the finest possible spatial detail. Downscaling products resolve a few hundred metres, whereas regional climate models resolve tens of kilometres. Finer is assumed better.

I tested this assumption.

4

So what drives it?

Not regional climate. A stand of trees dries out according to which way its slope faces and which species grow in it. Terrain and stand structure were the only inputs to reach positive skill, while adding climate actively dampened the result.

5

The part others can reuse

Before trusting any resolution comparison, it must clear two preconditions:

1. Skill gate

At least one model must beat simply guessing the average. Otherwise you are asking which of two broken clocks is more accurate.

2. Contrast gate

Coarsening must destroy a real share of the predictors' spatial detail. If it barely changes them, the two models are the same model.

Surrey failed gate 2 at 12.3%. That does not mean fine detail lost here. It means the comparison could not be made here.

6

Validation: one failed, one held

I checked the index two ways. Extending the record from four to nine summers broke the drought result, so I withdrew it. The ranking held: radar shows the index is tangled up with canopy density, but ranking each corridor by how dry it is for its own density gives nearly the same order (ρ = 0.92). Nothing has been checked on the ground, so the map stays exploratory.

The dry edge is a line fitted by comparing corridors to each other, at a slope of 0.53. Across summers the same two bands only move at 0.26. So when a dry summer pulls greenness down, the line drops faster than the corridor does, and the index scores that gap as water margin. Dry summers come out wetter.

7

What Surrey should do

Don't spend on finer climate data, spend on the site: soil, canopy and irrigation. What the measurement reliably delivers is coverage: all 144 corridors, including ones only 10 m wide, measured from free public satellite imagery and re‑ranked every summer, giving a fresh ranking each time, not a comparison between summers.
